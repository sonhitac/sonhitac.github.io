Equity, Diversity & Inclusion

Whose body gets drawn, and how

Representation in biomedical communication isn't neutral. Whose body is shown, whose visual literacy is assumed, and what symbol system is treated as universal: these are the choices that decide who a piece of science communication is actually for. I bring this practice of noticing into my teaching, research, mentorship, and editorial work.

In the classroom, I focus feedback on scientific reasoning rather than only the correctness of conclusions, and I adjust how directly I frame critique depending on a student's confidence with the material. I build accessibility into course materials as a baseline design requirement, applying standards consistent with the Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act (AODA): colour contrast, alternative text and formats, and multiple modalities for the same information. A visualization that fails an accessibility standard is, by this discipline's own logic, a communication failure, and I teach it that way.

"Biomedical illustration carries established, often unexamined visual conventions for skin tone, body type, age, gender identity, and ability. I've worked deliberately against that default." Sonhita Chakraborty

I've worked deliberately against that default in commissioned covers for Med (vol. 4, issue 6; vol. 7, issue 1) and Patterns (vol. 5, issue 12), and in Design by Hope, created for iGEM's first bioart gallery, choosing to depict people of colour rather than defaulting to a convention that often doesn't. As I build a research program on how metaphor is constructed and evaluated in biomedical communication, I want questions of representation, who is depicted, what an image assumes about a viewer's identity and prior knowledge, to be part of that evaluative framework itself.

As Associate Scientific Editor at Molecular Cell, I maintained the journal's reviewer tracking sheet, monitoring the diversity of reviewers invited to evaluate submitted manuscripts, and advocated for accessible, non-exclusionary language in the manuscripts I edited. In my current role as a strategic grant writer at the University of Toronto, I help faculty translate funding agencies' EDI expectations into a specific, substantive component of their research proposals, rather than a generic addition.

I place real value on being visibly available as a mentor. I've directly supervised three undergraduate research students, tailoring my guidance to each one's preparation and confidence rather than applying one standard approach, and I regularly respond to early-career scientists and illustrators who reach out for career guidance.

In practice, this means

  • Reviewer-diversity tracking as Associate Scientific Editor, Molecular Cell
  • AODA-consistent accessibility built into course materials by default
  • Commissioned covers (Med, Patterns) and Design by Hope choosing to depict people of colour rather than default convention
  • EDI components made specific and substantive, not generic, in the grant proposals I help write
  • Direct, individually tailored mentorship of undergraduate research students
