Equity, Diversity & Inclusion

Whose body gets drawn, and how

Representation in biomedical communication isn't neutral. Whose body is shown, whose visual literacy is assumed, and what symbol system is treated as universal: these are the choices that decide who a piece of science communication is actually for. I bring this practice of noticing into my teaching, research, mentorship, and editorial work.

Teaching and curriculum

I see inclusive teaching as making a field's unspoken assumptions visible, then giving students the tools to work beyond them. As a multilingual editor guiding authors who write in a second or third language, I have seen how much a term, idiom, or visual convention can assume about a reader when English is the default language of science. As a TA, I worked to make labs a place where students could be wrong out loud; one BIO230 student wrote that I made the lab "a positive space where mistakes were okay."

In my illustration workshops, I prompt participants to examine the assumptions in their visual choices, for example drawing a generic white male figure rather than a range of bodies when the message is how a disease affects people in general. In the BIO130 BioArt project at the University of Toronto, which my lab manual cover inspired, students show their understanding of the course material through their own visual metaphors and a written reflection, which gives students with different strengths and backgrounds more than one way to demonstrate what they know. At UTM, I would build this critique into BMC assignments, and I would make accessibility under the Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act (AODA) a baseline requirement (for example, palettes accessible to colour-vision deficiency, and alt text), because a visualization that fails an accessibility standard is, by its own logic, a communication failure.

"The field carries unexamined conventions for skin tone, body type, age, and ability." Sonhita Chakraborty

Research and representation

My commissioned covers for Patterns and Med have worked deliberately against that default by choosing to depict people of colour and celebrate Pride, respectively. In 2023, I brought my work to the community where I grew up with Deconstructing Biology, a solo exhibition at the Toronto Public Library of nine of my illustrations, journal covers among them, each paired with an explanation of its molecular processes for readers with no scientific training. At UTM, my research program would build representation into its comprehension studies, by recording participants' linguistic, cultural, and ability backgrounds (Stage 2), and into its fidelity framework, which includes who is depicted as a criterion (Stage 3).

Mentorship

Good mentorship makes the path into a field visible to students who do not yet see one for themselves, so I make a point of being visibly available. I have directly supervised three undergraduate research students and tailored my guidance to each one's preparation and confidence. I have also mentored incoming TAs as Head TA and spoken on career panels for students weighing careers in science communication, including the Perimeter Institute's "Inspiring Future Women in Science" conference for young women considering science careers. At UTM, I would mentor MSc students explicitly on the unspoken norms of funding, publishing, and networking.

Professional contributions to EDI

Inclusion must be built into the systems that decide whose work is published and funded. As Associate Scientific Editor at Molecular Cell, I monitored reviewer diversity on the journal's tracking sheet and advocated for accessible, non-exclusionary manuscript language. As a Strategic Grant Writer at the University of Toronto, I help faculty make EDI a specific, substantive part of their research plans. As a graduate student, I served as Ombudsperson and CUPE representative for graduate students in my department. At UTM, I would bring the same habit of checking who is in the room to departmental service and curriculum review.

Future commitments at BMC

Three commitments follow directly from this work. First, every course I teach would include a representation audit, in which students examine a set of published biomedical images for who is shown as the default body and redesign one image for a broader audience. Second, I would recruit lay participants for my comprehension studies through community partners such as Toronto and Mississauga public libraries, not only university pools, so that the "public" in my results reflects the communities BMC graduates will serve. Third, I would collect anonymous mid-term feedback in every course and tell students what I changed in response.

In practice

  • Reviewer-diversity tracking at Molecular Cell, and advocacy for accessible, non-exclusionary manuscript language
  • Accessibility under the AODA as a baseline for student work
  • A representation audit in every course I would teach
  • Lay participants recruited through community partners such as Toronto and Mississauga public libraries
  • Anonymous mid-term feedback in every course, with a report back on what changed
  • Mentorship of undergraduate researchers, incoming TAs, and career-panel audiences
