Research

My research asks whether experts and the public understand the same visual metaphors for biomedical concepts in the same way, and how biomedical images should be designed once we know.

Where the question comes from

Foundations

Signals in cells, signals in images

My doctoral research (Cell & Systems Biology, University of Toronto) examined the plant ion channel CNGC2 and its role in auxin signalling, the hormonal system that coordinates plant growth (Chakraborty et al., 2021). My postdoctoral work at the Umeå Plant Science Centre in Sweden examined how temperature stress affects plant cell-to-cell communication. Both projects asked how a biological signal moves, degrades, or is reinterpreted as conditions change. A visual metaphor is also a signal: it travels from creator to audience, and it can convey the science faithfully to one audience, oversimplify it for another, and mislead a third.

Editor, illustrator, and co-investigator

Since 2021 I have also worked as a journal editor and as a science illustrator. As Associate Scientific Editor at Molecular Cell, I assessed proposed cover art against the standard every editor applies: is it compelling, accurate, and clear to its intended audience? I ask the same questions as a commissioned illustrator, and as the artist behind Deconstructing Biology (2023), a Toronto Public Library exhibition that explained the science behind nine of my illustrations to visitors with no scientific training.

At the University of Toronto, my BIO130 lab manual cover (2022–2024) inspired an experiential learning program, launched by the course teaching team in 2024, in which students create visual metaphors for course concepts. I am now co-investigator, with principal investigator Dr. Adriana Caragea, on an ethics-approved study of that project, which examines how students translate biological concepts into visual metaphor and how that relates to their learning.

The gap

Visual metaphor has been studied in advertising and cartoons and catalogued in science infographics and graphical abstracts, but how it is interpreted across audiences remains underexplored. Biology experts organize molecular-biology representations by conceptual features, whereas novices rely more on surface features such as colour and visual style (Newman et al., 2023), and one experiment found that metaphor increased perceived, but not actual, comprehension (Reijnierse et al., 2025). To my knowledge, no study has compared specialist conventions, everyday metaphors, and literal renderings of the same biomedical concept across expert and lay viewers while measuring understanding.

The program

Three stages, five years

Each stage answers one question and sets up the next, and I would involve one MSc student in each.

Stage 1. Mapping the metaphor (Years 1–2)

Which visual metaphors and conventions circulate in biomedical editorial imagery? I will build a taxonomy of the metaphors in my own commissioned work, a broader sample of scientific journal covers, and a parallel sample of editorial illustration from science media for non-specialists. Using a codebook grounded in conceptual metaphor theory, I will code each image for subject domain, source domain, narrative form, and degree of abstraction, and I will interview editors and art directors about the editorial decisions behind published images.

A first pass coding my 13 published cover images found that 11 borrow from at least one non-scientific source domain (everyday objects, the natural world, or culturally specific imagery), including all 6 that address human health or disease. Even specialist-facing images, in other words, lean heavily on everyday and cultural metaphor.

Stage 2. Testing comprehension (Years 2–4)

Do expert and lay audiences understand them the same way? I will render matched biomedical concepts three ways, using a specialist convention, an everyday metaphor, and a literal diagram, with palette, composition, and level of detail held constant. Matched expert and lay groups will see the same concept drawn each way, and I will measure recall, inference, and confidence a week later. Because I create the stimuli, outside scientists will vet them for accuracy, and because comprehension methods lie outside my training, I would design these studies with a collaborator in psychology or language comprehension. Where recruitment allows, I will also record participants' linguistic, cultural, and ability backgrounds.

Stage 3. A fidelity framework (Years 4–5)

How should communicative power be weighed against scientific fidelity? Stages 1 and 2 converge on a practitioner-usable framework: a short set of criteria (for example, structural accuracy, audience fit, who is depicted, and promotional versus instructive purpose) with a simple rating scale. I will test it by asking two independent groups of practicing editors and illustrators to score the same pieces, while a separate panel of scientists or clinicians assesses accuracy.

Funding and collaboration

As a Strategic Grant Writer, I advise University of Toronto faculty on CIHR, NSERC, and SSHRC applications, and I would build each of my own applications on the results of the last. A UTM Research Seed Grant or a Connaught New Researcher Award would support the mapping stage and a pilot comprehension study, followed by an SSHRC Insight Development Grant and an Insight Grant, an SSHRC Partnership Engage Grant with a journal publisher to test the framework in editorial practice, and CIHR funding with a health-communication partner as a longer-term goal. Each stage is planned to yield a paper, and I would present the work at Visualizing Biological Data (VIZBI) and the Association of Medical Illustrators conference. In return for collaborators' methods expertise, I can offer scientific vetting of molecular and cell-biology content, an editor's view of how images fare in peer review, and experience preparing tri-agency grant applications.
