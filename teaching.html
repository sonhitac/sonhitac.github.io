Teaching

I treat visual and scientific communication as a skill built through iteration, not a fixed talent some people have and others don't.

Teaching philosophy

As a science editor, I spent years giving writers structured, developmental feedback before and after peer review, and I bring that same cycle of draft, critique, and revision into the classroom.

(I taught as a teaching assistant at the University of Toronto from [2016 to 2021], including as Head TA for a 200-level course.)

I also teach representation as a series of consequential choices. Every visual or written simplification of a biological process foregrounds some features and hides others, and a finished figure isn't self-evidently correct just because it looks polished.

"When a student's diagram of a signaling pathway compresses several steps into one arrow, the question isn't whether the image is 'accurate' in the abstract. It's what the compression cost, whether that was a deliberate choice or a habit, and whether the student can defend it to an audience who doesn't already know the answer." Sonhita Chakraborty, on teaching representation

That question, not the image itself, is what I'm actually teaching, and it's the same question my research investigates.

My approach to assessment comes directly from editorial practice: clarity, accuracy, and impact, used as an explicit, shared rubric rather than an implicit standard only the instructor can see.

Working with Biomedical Communications students

Meeting the actual gap

Students in this field arrive from genuinely different starting points, some with a biology degree and little formal visual training, others with an arts background and less confidence in scientific content. Having occupied both of those positions myself across a career spanning molecular biology, science editing, and commissioned illustration, I aim to meet students where their gap actually is rather than teach to an assumed average. In critique, I focus feedback on whether a piece of communication works for its intended audience, not on taste, and I build accessibility (legibility for colorblind viewers, screen readers, and varied print conditions) into design instruction as a baseline constraint rather than an optional add-on.

Equity in the studio

A short note on inclusive teaching

I build accessibility into course materials as a baseline design requirement, applying standards consistent with the Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act (AODA), not as an optional add-on. A visualization that fails an accessibility standard is, by this discipline's own logic, a communication failure, and I teach it that way.
