Teaching

I teach visual and scientific communication as a skill that improves through revision, not a talent students either have or lack, with process before polish.

Teaching philosophy

I learned it that way, as a scientist who became an editor and illustrator, and I teach it the same way. As an editor, I learned that feedback helps most when it is specific and tells the writer what to do next. In a course, I would run critique as a shared diagnostic exercise, in which the group first states what a peer's image is trying to communicate and only then discusses whether it succeeds. Students would learn to give feedback as well as receive it, and to treat early drafts as the place to test ideas.

I also treat representation as a series of consequential choices. Every simplification of a biological process shows some features and leaves others out.

"When a student compresses several steps of a signalling pathway into a single arrow, I would ask what the arrow hides and whether that was intended." Sonhita Chakraborty, on teaching representation

The BIO130 BioArt project, which grew out of the lab manual cover I created for the course, builds this habit into first-year biology. When students translate a course concept into a visual metaphor, they must make deliberate choices about what to include or omit, then reflect on how those choices shape the final result. As co-investigator on an ethics-approved study of the project, I will examine how making these choices relates to learning, a question central to my research.

My approach to assessment comes from editing, where I organize feedback around accuracy, clarity, and impact. In a scientific illustration, accuracy means the science is correct, clarity means the intended audience understands the message, and impact means the design directs attention to what matters and shows why. I would build these criteria into every rubric so students can judge their own work, and I would grade drafts and revisions, not only finished pieces.

Teaching experience

Five years in University of Toronto biology labs

I was a teaching assistant at the University of Toronto from 2016 to 2021 in BIO130 Molecular and Cell Biology (first year, about 1,800 students a year), BIO230 From Genes to Organisms (second year), and CSB353 Introduction to Plant-Microbe Interactions. In my first year as a BIO130 TA, students rated my overall impression 4.9 out of 5, against a course TA average of 4.5. In BIO230, I scored 4.64 out of 5 for overall impression and 4.89 for responsiveness to student questions.

As Head TA for BIO230 (Fall 2019 and Fall 2020), I trained and supervised the course's TAs, revised lab notes, quizzes, and pre-lab presentations for clarity, and added animations of each lab's procedures. I ran focus groups with the TAs that shaped the redesign of the course's annotated-bibliography assignment, and in 2020 I adapted the labs for online delivery and prepared 30 TAs to teach online for the first time.

Since 2023, I have also taught short workshops and talks on visual and written science communication, from first-year undergraduates to postdoctoral researchers, including a graphical-abstract workshop for The Company of Biologists (2025) and "Journeys into Science Art" for BIO130 (2026). I have directly supervised three undergraduate research students. The CV has the full list.

Working with Biomedical Communications students

Meeting the actual gap

Students in Biomedical Communications (BMC) come from different backgrounds. Some have biology training but little visual experience, while others come from the arts and are less confident with science. My own path through molecular biology, science editing, and commissioned illustration has required me to move between these ways of thinking, and I would teach students to do the same. I would form critique groups that mix scientific and visual expertise, so students learn from each other's strengths as well as from me.

As Head TA for BIO230, I redesigned the pre-lab presentations and added animations of each lab's procedures, my first experience designing visuals for teaching. Five years of teaching first- and second-year biology labs have also prepared me to teach undergraduates in the BMC minor.

In supervision, I favour an apprenticeship model. My three undergraduate researchers worked on open questions within my doctoral research, and I would give each MSc student a distinct question within one of the three stages of my proposed research. Because graduate supervision is new for me, I would begin as a co-supervisor or committee member alongside experienced BMC faculty.

Courses

What I'd teach

Courses in the existing BMC and HSC curriculum where I could contribute from the start, and two I would develop.

Could teach immediately

  • MSC2004H, Research Methods. My editing and grant-writing work centres on the documents this course teaches, including literature reviews, proposals, and research papers.
  • MSC2001Y, Visual Representation of Medical Knowledge (as a co-instructor or contributor). The course covers textbook and journal illustration, which is the core of my commissioned practice and of my editorial work assessing cover art at Molecular Cell.
  • HSC302H5, Biocommunication Visualization. The course asks how visual media for biological topics handle scale, scientific conventions, and audience, the same questions behind my research and my journal-cover practice.
  • HSC308H5, Visual Methods: From Atoms to Cells. My PhD in cell and systems biology, my work as Associate Scientific Editor at Molecular Cell, and my covers for journals including Cell, Molecular Cell, and Cell Metabolism all centre on visualizing molecular and cellular processes.
  • HSC300H5, Written Communication for Health Care. Five years of professional science editing at Elsevier, Molecular Cell, The Plant Cell, and Life Science Editors have centred on making research findings accurate and readable for audiences with different levels of expertise.

I could also contribute to HSC200H5, Imaging Technologies for Scientific Visual Communication, and HSC307H5, Visual Presentation Design for Life Sciences, drawing on my commissioned illustration and graphical-abstract workshops. Within existing studio courses, I could serve as a molecular and cell biology content resource for students' visualization projects.

Would develop from here

  • Visual Metaphor and Conceptual Translation in Biomedical Communication. An elective built from my research on how metaphor shapes the way audiences understand biomedical concepts. It would extend the information-design foundation of HSC301H5 into a more theory-driven treatment of metaphor and abstraction, and could first run as MSC2011H, Special Topics in Biomedical Communications. Assignments would adapt the BIO130 BioArt model for a graduate audience. In one proposed assignment (not yet taught), students choose a published cover or graphical abstract, identify what its metaphor foregrounds and hides, redesign it for a different audience, and test both versions with a few viewers from that audience.
  • Science Publishing and Editorial Practicum. A project-based course in which students respond to simulated commissioning briefs, modelled on the editorial process I have worked in from both sides, as illustrator and as editor. It would complement the existing written-communication courses (HSC300H5 and HSC406H5) and the professional-practice content of MSC2009H by focusing on how journals commission, judge, and use images and text.
Equity in the studio

A short note on inclusive teaching

I would make accessibility under the Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act (AODA) a baseline requirement for student work (for example, palettes accessible to colour-vision deficiency, and alt text), because a visualization that fails an accessibility standard is, by its own logic, a communication failure.
